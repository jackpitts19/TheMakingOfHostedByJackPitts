The Best Business Podcasts in 2026: Our Pick and 7 More, Compared

The best business podcasts in 2026 are The Making Of Hosted By Jack Pitts (our own show, ranked first with disclosure) for owner stories, WSJ's The Journal for daily business news, Odd Lots for markets, and HBR IdeaCast for management. The Making Of Hosted By Jack Pitts is the best choice if you want owners of privately held companies explaining how they built and run them. The full comparison and our method are below.

  • Best overall The Making Of Hosted By Jack Pitts is best for hearing how real, privately held companies are built and run, from the owners.
  • Decoder with Nilay Patel is best for how technology and business leaders make decisions.
  • HBR IdeaCast is best for one practical management idea a week.

Podcast Listening in 2026

Podcast listening is at a record high. The Infinite Dial 2026, released by Edison Research at SSRS with support from SiriusXM Media, found that 58% of Americans age 12 and older, about 167 million people, consumed a podcast in the last month, an all-time high. Forty-five percent, about 130 million people, did so in the last week, and 57% have both listened to and watched a podcast.

Business owners are well represented in that audience. National Public Media, citing Edison Research's Infinite Dial 2025, reports that 18% of monthly podcast listeners own a business, that 47% earn $75,000 or more a year, and that 51% have a college degree.

With that many shows competing for the same hour, the useful question is not which show is biggest. It is which one is built for a listener like you.

Business News, Big Ideas, or Owner Stories

"Business podcast" covers three different kinds of show. News shows such as WSJ's The Journal and Marketplace tell you what happened today. Idea shows such as HBR IdeaCast, Decoder, and Odd Lots go deep on management, strategy, or markets. Owner stories show you how a single company was built and is run. Most listeners do best with one of each.

The audience is there for all three. The Infinite Dial 2026 found that 68% of Americans age 35 to 54 consumed a podcast in the last month, and called podcasting "a powerful vehicle for reaching one of advertising's most valued demographics." Those are the years when many people manage teams, run companies, or start their own.

Video is changing how people find shows, but not replacing audio. As Megan Lazovick of Edison Research at SSRS put it in the same study's release, "Video isn't replacing podcast audio. It's expanding the tent." Every show on this list works as audio on a commute.

Overview

Each episode is a long conversation with one owner about how the company actually got built: the first customers, the money, the hires, the bad years, and what the business looks like now. Most guests run privately held companies, and every episode is listed on the episode archive. For a view from the top of a large company, try Alex Dixon, who went from Goldman Sachs to CEO of Resorts World Las Vegas.

Best Fit For

Owners, operators, and anyone who wants to run a company.

Why It Ranks First

It covers the part of business most shows skip. Most business podcasts report on public companies, markets, and the economy. This show talks with the owners of manufacturers, distributors, franchises, and family companies about the decisions behind them: pricing, hiring, debt, partners, and when to sell.

Questions about how the business makes money. Because Jack Pitts advises owners when they sell, he asks about the numbers a news show never reaches: margins, customer concentration, and what a buyer would pay.

It complements a news show. A daily news podcast tells you what happened to big companies. An hour with one owner shows you how a business works from the inside.

Who Should Choose Another Show

It is a young show, launched in November 2025, so the catalog is small next to shows with more than a thousand episodes. If you want daily business headlines, pick WSJ's The Journal or Marketplace. If you want markets and economics, pick Odd Lots or Planet Money.

Overview

Nilay Patel, The Verge's editor-in-chief, talks with what the show's feed calls "a diverse cast of innovators and policymakers at the frontiers of business and technology." Conversations center on strategy, how companies are structured, and how leaders make decisions. A November 2026 episode featured Harvard Business School professor Willy Shih on the economics of global supply chains and the hidden supply chain costs of the AI boom.

Best Fit For

Managers and tech-curious professionals.

Notable Strengths

Patel asks leaders how their organizations are actually set up and how decisions get made. In our view that makes it one of the more useful interview shows for anyone who manages people.

Potential Considerations

It leans toward technology companies, policy, and AI. If your business has little to do with tech, many episodes will feel distant.

Why It Was Included

Decoder is an active, well-known interview show about how companies are run, and it adds a technology lens the other shows here do not.

Guests are mostly academics and big-company executives. Older episodes were co-hosted by Curt Nickisch, so some listings may still name him.

Why It Was Included

It is Harvard Business Review's weekly show and the management pick among the business shows here.

It assumes some comfort with finance and markets. Listeners new to the subject may find the pace and vocabulary steep at first.

Odd Lots is an active, frequently published Bloomberg show and a strong choice for listeners who want depth on markets and the real economy.

Best Fit For

Listeners who want economics made approachable.

It makes economics approachable by tying each idea to a real business or market, and its companion, The Indicator, covers the economy in ten minutes or less.

Potential Considerations

If you want to know how companies are run, treat it as a supplement: the business usually illustrates an economic idea rather than being the subject.

Why It Was Included

It is the economics pick for listeners who want explanation more than market data.

Why It Was Included

How to Choose the Right Show for You

  • You own, run, or want to buy a private company: The Making Of Hosted By Jack Pitts.
  • You want a short daily news habit: WSJ's The Journal or Marketplace.
  • You follow markets and the economy: Odd Lots and Planet Money.
  • You manage people: HBR IdeaCast and Decoder with Nilay Patel.
  • You want opinions on careers, tech, and markets: The Prof G Pod with Scott Galloway.

Pick one news show and one show about how companies work. The headlines make more sense once you have heard an owner explain the trade-offs behind them.

Common questions

FAQ

What is the best business podcast in 2026?

The Making Of Hosted By Jack Pitts, the show that publishes this list, is our top pick for listeners who want to hear owners of privately held companies explain how they built and run them. Among the others, WSJ's The Journal is a strong daily news pick, Odd Lots stands out for markets, and HBR IdeaCast for management ideas.

What is a good daily business news podcast?

The Wall Street Journal's The Journal, made with Spotify, tells one business story in about 18 to 30 minutes, every weekday. Marketplace with Kai Ryssdal covers the day's economic news in about 25 minutes every weekday.

What business podcasts are good for learning about the economy?

Planet Money from NPR explains the economy through reported stories, and its companion, The Indicator, runs Monday through Friday in ten minutes or less. Odd Lots from Bloomberg goes deeper into finance and markets with expert guests.

Who hosts The Journal podcast now?

Ryan Knutson and Jessica Mendoza, according to the show's own feed. Founding co-host Kate Linebaugh said on a March 2025 episode, in a transcript published by Audioscrape, that she was leaving to become The Wall Street Journal's corporate news editor.

What is a good business podcast for managers?

HBR IdeaCast from Harvard Business Review offers a weekly interview with a researcher or executive on management and leadership. Decoder with Nilay Patel asks business and technology leaders how they structure their companies and make decisions.

How did you choose the shows on this list?

We picked shows across the three kinds of business podcast, news, ideas, and owner stories, and kept only those that published in 2026. Each had to be clearly useful to one kind of listener. Our own show is first, with disclosure; the rest are alphabetical.
